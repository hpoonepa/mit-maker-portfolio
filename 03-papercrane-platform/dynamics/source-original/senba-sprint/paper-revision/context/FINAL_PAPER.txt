                                                      Abstract

1             When a molecular simulation foundation model is adapted to a new objective, the
2             size of the reported improvement depends on two settings that such papers rarely
3             sweep and often do not state: how far the weights are moved from the source
4             checkpoint, and how many stochastic samples are averaged before scoring. We
5             measure both for one deliberately small adaptation of M AR S-FM: ﬁne-tune on
6             same-replica mdCATH frame pairs separated by exactly 50 saved frames, then
7             keep only 7.35% of the learned weight displacement by interpolating back toward
8             the pinned source. On the complete eligible 28-protein validation panel this lowers
9             ensemble-mean endpoint Cα RMSD by 0.03259 Å (27/28 proteins improve), and
10            passes a prespeciﬁed seven-observable retention screen that every more aggressive
11            adaptation we tried failed. A separate frozen-checkpoint evaluator then extends
12            sampling from K = 4 to K = 16 draws on eight of those proteins. Endpoint
13            error stays lower on 8/8 proteins at every K, but the mean gain falls 42.4%, from
14            0.02401 to 0.01383 Å. Decomposing that error explains why: the shrinking part is
15            a 1/K dispersion term, while the K-independent location component moves the
16            other way, strengthening from 0.09496 to 0.22590 Å2 . A strictly proper energy
17            score, whose estimator targets a K-independent quantity, favors the candidate at
18            every K. The full panel also shows an improvement in the direction of predicted
19            displacement (25/28 proteins, sign test p = 2.7 × 10−5 ), which contraction of
20            the ensemble cannot by itself produce. The adaptation is real and smaller than a
21            single-K number suggests. We report the surface rather than its maximum, on the
22            view that a sub-angstrom claim in this area is uninterpretable until the ensemble
23            size and anchoring strength that produced it are stated alongside it. All evidence is
24            validation-only at one lag and 450 K; the standard test partition was never opened.


25   1 Introduction

26   Improvements reported for machine-learned molecular dynamics are usually small in absolute
27   terms—fractions of an angstrom against errors of ten or more—and are read across papers as though
28   they were properties of the model. They are not. A reported gain is a coordinate in an evaluation
29   space: it depends on how far the adapted weights were permitted to move from the source, on how
30   many stochastic samples were averaged before scoring, and on which of several defensible error
31   functionals was reported. The ﬁrst two are rarely swept and often not stated; the third is usually cho-
32   sen once. This paper takes one deliberately small adaptation of a molecular simulation foundation
33   model and measures its own result along all three axes, on a ﬁxed panel, from hash-pinned receipts,
34   with the standard test partition unopened.

35   The source model, M AR S-FM [Kapuśniak et al., 2026], generates conformations by learning transi-
36   tions across states supplied by a Markov-state construction rather than by ﬁtting one ﬁxed temporal
37   offset. That suits broad conformational generation but does not optimize prediction of a particu-
38   lar observed displacement, and direct supervision at a single lag can specialize the model to that
39   endpoint while moving it away from source behavior that matters for ensemble sampling. Our ques-
40   tion is narrow: can a small, explicitly source-anchored weight update improve exact-lag endpoint
41   prediction without breaching prespeciﬁed retention limits?
42   Weight interpolation between a pretrained source and a ﬁne-tuned model is an established robust-
43   ness device in vision and language [Wortsman et al., 2022a,b; Ilharco et al., 2023], and it has been
44   benchmarked for molecular graph foundation models [Liu et al., 2025]. What is not established is
45   the corresponding question for a generative simulator: whether a coefﬁcient chosen to protect end-
46   point accuracy also protects the ensemble observables the source was valued for, and whether the
47   resulting gain survives being measured properly. We treat interpolation as a transparent control on
48   parameter displacement, not as a guarantee.
49   The setting is mdCATH, a collection of all-atom molecular dynamics trajectories for protein domains
50   with multiple replicas and temperatures [Mirarchi et al., 2024]. We use a frozen 128-domain training
51   subset and 32-domain validation subset at 450 K, and evaluate on every validation domain eligible
52   under the source model’s 256-residue crop.

53   Contributions.

54         1. A sampling-size sensitivity result, not a point estimate. The same frozen checkpoint is
55            evaluated at K = 4, 8 and 16 draws on the same 160 transitions with preﬁx-preserving
56            nested ensembles. The endpoint gain falls 42.4%, from 0.02401 to 0.01383 Å, while all
57            eight proteins remain improved at every K.
58         2. A decomposition that separates the artifact from the effect. Splitting endpoint error into
59            location and 1/K dispersion terms shows the shrinking component is ﬁnite-ensemble bias,
60            while the K-independent location improvement more than doubles over the same sweep.
61            A strictly proper energy score [Gneiting and Raftery, 2007] and four previously unreported
62            full-panel metrics—including displacement direction, which ensemble contraction cannot
63            improve—agree with the corrected term.
64         3. An adaptation-distance ladder with retention gates attached. Full ﬁne-tuning, output-
65            head-only adaptation, 25% and 50% objective mixtures, and α ∈ {0.10, 0.20} all improve
66            exact-lag prediction yet fail a prespeciﬁed seven-observable retention screen; α = 0.0735
67            passes all seven.
68         4. Negative results and an auditable evidence boundary. We report the failures, the off-grid
69            selection of α, and one published interval that our own audit could not reproduce.

89    3 Source-anchored exact-lag adaptation
90    3.1   Exact-lag parent training

91    For each training domain the loader reads all ﬁve 450 K replicas. Starting-state cluster labels are
92    used only to balance sampling: clusters are sampled uniformly, and an equal number of observed
93    starts is drawn from each selected cluster, with replacement when required. Each sampled pair
94    comes from one replica and is separated by exactly 50 frames, so direct endpoint supervision cannot
95    cross replica boundaries or substitute a Markov-state draw for the observed future.
96    The seed-1 parent begins from the source checkpoint and updates all 34,152,521 parameters. It
97    trains for 10 epochs and 160 optimizer updates over 30,720 sampled transitions, with 8 domains per
98    batch and 24 transitions per domain (192 transition pairs per update), at learning rate 3 × 10−6 . A
99    ﬁxed validation transition population is materialized once and ﬂow-matching randomness replayed,
100   so validation losses are paired across epochs; the best checkpoint is selected by minimum validation
101   loss. The run took 121.80 s on one NVIDIA H100—a measurement of the selected run, not the
102   search campaign.

103   3.2   Source anchoring

104   Let θ0 denote the source weights and θp the ﬁne-tuned parent. The evaluated candidate is
                                    θα = θ0 + α(θp − θ0 ),       α = 0.0735.                              (1)
105   Floating tensors are interpolated in their stored dtype; non-ﬂoating state must match exactly. No
106   gradient update follows interpolation, so the candidate retains 7.35% of the parent’s learned dis-
107   placement and remains a single model at inference. Equation (1) is a scaled task vector [Ilharco et
108   al., 2023], the simplest member of a family that includes sign- and magnitude-aware merging [Yadav
109   et al., 2023]; we did not try those, and a uniform scalar α treats every parameter identically.
110   The coefﬁcient is a development-selected operating point, not a physically meaningful pa-
111   rameter. It also lies outside the pre-registered epoch-sweep anchor grid, which speciﬁed
112   {0.02, 0.03, 0.04, 0.06} for the ten-epoch parent; 0.0735 came from a subsequent anchor reﬁnement
113   on the same previously inspected validation evidence. That is an additional selection step beyond
114   the frozen grid, and one more reason the coefﬁcient must not be read as prospectively validated.
115   Section 5.5 reports what happens on either side of it.

131   4.2   Sampling-aware frozen-checkpoint evaluator

132   The second evaluator is a new implementation, not a reproduction of the original, which is unavail-
133   able. We state this plainly because it bounds what the comparison can show. It evaluates the ﬁxed
134   released checkpoint on the eight-domain compact panel, using all ﬁve 450 K replicas and starts
135   ⌊i(N − 51)/3⌋ for i = 0, . . . , 3, with targets 50 saved frames later—160 paired transitions. Hash-
136   veriﬁed HDF5 inputs are mapped to complete heavy-atom structures in ﬂoat32 angstroms. Source
137   and candidate use ten ODE steps and identical paired random streams.
138   Sampling is extended preﬁx-preservingly: for transition index j the original K = 4 block uses seed
139   20260904 + j, and three further blocks use that seed plus b × 106 for b = 1, 2, 3. K = 8 joins blocks
140   0–1 and K = 16 all four, so the original draws survive exactly inside every larger ensemble. The
141   extension was speciﬁed before its outputs were computed and the panel never changed. Intervals use
142   2,000 protein bootstrap resamples. Because the three ensembles share the same 160 transitions
143   and nest, the K estimates are not independent replications, and their intervals are conditional.
144   Alongside endpoint RMSD we report the energy score [Gneiting and Raftery, 2007; Gneiting et al.,
145   2008], the strictly proper multivariate generalization of CRPS, in its standard ﬁnite-ensemble form:
146   mean sample-to-target distance minus half the mean off-diagonal sample-pair distance, unsquared.
147   Strict propriety matters here—unlike a mean-based error it cannot be improved by under-dispersing
148   alone. We also use sample MSE = centroid MSE + ensemble variance and the ﬁnite-K diagnos-
149   tic centroid MSE − ensemble variance/(K − 1), which under i.i.d. draws corrects Monte Carlo
150   variance against one observed target but not target uncertainty or biological bias.

151   4.3   Ensemble-retention protocol

152   The retention suite compares candidate and source on the eight-protein panel. For each protein and
153   checkpoint the breadth-ﬁrst generation tree uses breadth 200, retains 500 samples in chunks of 100,
154   uses 50 ODE steps, and starts from the same frame and seed; references use all frames from all ﬁve
155   450 K replicas. Generated samples are unordered ensemble draws, not a temporal trajectory. Seven
156   observables are evaluated after common-frame Cα alignment: DCCM mean absolute error, native-
157   contact-fraction and pairwise-RMSD Wasserstein-1 distances, PCA-subspace RMSIP, prediction
158   clash fraction, radius-of-gyration Wasserstein-1 distance, and RMSF RMSE. The gate was ﬁxed
159   before the aggregate: for each observable the upper interval endpoint for mean degradation must not
160   exceed a preset limit, at least six of eight proteins must be within it, and no protein may exceed twice
161   it. All seven must pass. This is a non-inferiority-style development screen with ﬁxed tolerances—not
162   an equivalence test, and not evidence that the ensemble is physically correct.

184   5.2   Ensemble retention

185   All seven retention observables pass their frozen rules, each within limit on 8/8 proteins rather than
186   the required 6/8 (Appendix C). Six mean degradations are negative or near zero; the largest is PCA-

203   The energy score behaves differently, and the difference is instructive rather than contradictory. Its
204   estimated gain is 0.00328, 0.00604 and 0.00642 Å at K = 4, 8 and 16. We do not read this as the
205   score improving with K: the ﬁnite-ensemble estimator targets a K-independent population quantity,
206   so these are three estimates of one number, and only their precision changed—non-monotonically,
207   since the K = 16 interval is the widest of the three (0.01006 Å) and the K = 8 interval the
208   narrowest (0.00482 Å). The correct statement is that a strictly proper score favors the candidate at
209   every K while the raw endpoint gain does not hold still.


210   The disagreement is not a contradiction. For K i.i.d. draws the expected squared error of an
211   ensemble mean carries a 1/K dispersion term on top of the location error, so two checkpoints
212   whose ensemble variances differ differ in centroid MSE by a term decaying like 1/K—and the
213   candidate is consistently less dispersive. Table 2 separates them additively: the raw centroid-MSE
214   gain decays from 0.57499 to 0.35058 Å2 while the Monte Carlo part ∆σ 2 /(K−1) decays faster,
215   0.48003 to 0.12468 Å2 . The spread-corrected location term moves the other way, strengthening
216   from 0.09496 to 0.22590 Å2 and excluding zero from K = 8. Fitting MSE(K) = B + V /K
217   to the stored per-transition arrays puts the intercept-space gain at 0.22593 Å2 , matching the ﬁnite-
218   K corrected estimator (0.22590) to four decimal places; we quote the asymptote in MSE space
219   only, because translating it into RMSD units depends on whether the square root is taken before
220   or after aggregation (0.0103 versus 0.0082 Å). So at K = 4 a substantial share of the apparent
221   endpoint gain was a dispersion artifact; at K = 16 what remains is a location improvement. The
222   two K-invariant summaries—the corrected diagnostic and the strictly proper energy score—favor
223   the candidate at every K, though neither becomes more precise monotonically. This is the paper’s
224   central measurement, and it is why we do not report the K = 4 number alone.

225   5.4   Per-protein consistency and spread

226   All eight proteins retain lower centroid endpoint RMSD at K = 16, from −0.00078 Å (2k4qA00) to
227   −0.04083 Å (1q16B03) (Figure 1, right). The energy score improves on seven; 2k4qA00 regresses
228   by +0.00505 Å and is retained in every aggregate.
229   Candidate ensemble variance and pairwise RMSD are lower on all eight domains (−1.87020 Å2
230   and −0.11302 Å at K = 16). Neither direction is intrinsically favorable: a narrower ensemble can
231   lower some endpoint scores without recovering the correct distribution. We report it because the
232   energy score would otherwise be uninterpretable—being strictly proper, it cannot be improved by
233   under-dispersion alone, which is why its agreement with the corrected location term, and with the
234   direction-cosine result of Section 5.1, carries information.

235   5.5   Negative and contrary results

236   These outcomes are ﬁndings, not caveats, and they are what ﬁxes the operating point.
237   A fully exact-lag parent, output-head-only adaptations, and 25% and 50% objective mixtures all
238   improved transition prediction more than conservative anchors, yet failed the frozen retention limits.
239   An MSM-only control retained ensemble behavior but showed no supported endpoint gain. Among
240   earlier anchors, α = 0.10 missed one limit and α = 0.20 missed several. Maximizing exact-lag
241   improvement without a separate retention constraint is therefore ruled out on this panel.
242   A 7.5% interpolation failed the one-protein equilibrium pilot where 7.35% passed. We do not present
243   this as a located threshold: it is a one-protein pilot against an eight-protein pass, the 0.15-percentage-
244   point separation is within plausible single-protein Monte Carlo variation, and our own internal re-
245   view ﬂagged it as such. It shows sensitivity to development selection and argues for evaluating
246   anchors jointly on larger untouched cohorts.
247   The candidate is not uniformly superior to the earlier anchored checkpoint: two seeds favor it while
248   seed 2 differs by −0.00110 Å with an interval crossing zero. Consistent advantage over the public
249   source does not imply advantage over every nearby development checkpoint.

250   6 Discussion

251   Controlling source distance can preserve a transition gain that survives predeﬁned ensemble screens.
252   It does not show that linear interpolation is optimal, that α = 0.0735 transfers, or that passing source-
253   relative observables ensures physical validity—the suite protects selected properties of the source,
254   so any systematic source error is inherited. The ﬁeld’s standard for a physically correct ensemble is
255   Boltzmann reweighting [Noé et al., 2019], which we neither attempt nor meet.
256   The sampling result is the part we expect to travel. That ﬁnite-ensemble scores are biased in en-
257   semble size is known in probabilistic forecasting [Ferro et al., 2008; Ferro, 2014]; what we add is
258   a worked instance in molecular simulation where the bias consumes 42% of a reported effect, and
259   the decomposition that recovers the invariant part. Concretely: report a K-curve with nested preﬁx-
260   preserving draws, a distribution-sensitive score alongside the mean-based one, and the spread, so
261   improvements can be attributed rather than assumed. This costs one re-scoring pass over samples
262   already generated. We show it for one checkpoint on one panel; we do not claim other published
263   gains are artifacts.

264   Outlook. Four directions follow. First, the α × K surface should be measured prospectively on
265   an untouched cohort with the anchor grid preregistered, converting the retention frontier from a de-
266   velopment artifact into a transferable operating curve. Second, curvature- and sign-aware anchoring
267   [Kirkpatrick et al., 2017; Li et al., 2018; Yadav et al., 2023] would test whether the usable window
268   is genuinely under 8% of the displacement or an artifact of treating all parameters alike. Third,
269   specializing to a single lag may degrade lag-composition consistency that a Markov-state objective
270   encourages implicitly, and static ensemble observables cannot detect that; the decisive test is cheap
271   and we did not run it—apply the model once at lag 100 versus twice at lag 50 and check Chapman–
272   Kolmogorov agreement. Fourth, and most consequential if it holds, the decomposition is not speciﬁc
273   to molecular dynamics: any generative model scored by averaging stochastic draws against a single

                                                          7
274   observed outcome inherits the same 1/K term, so the same correction applies to conformational en-
275   sembles, docking poses, and trajectory prediction generally. Establishing whether adaptation gains
276   reported in those settings survive the same treatment is the experiment we would run next, and it
277   requires no new training.


278   7 Limitations

279   Validation reuse. Selection and evaluation used previously inspected validation data. The 28-
280   protein panel is exhaustive for the frozen length-eligible population but not untouched, and the
281   intervals describe variation among these proteins, not post-selection coverage. One lag and one
282   condition. All scored transitions use one 50-frame separation at 450 K. There is no multi-lag or
283   semigroup consistency test [Schreiner et al., 2023], no kinetic estimate, no calibrated physical time,
284   and no rare-event evaluation; endpoints are ﬁnite-window Cα samples, not trajectories. Restricted
285   proteins and coordinates. Scoring is Cα -based on 28 domains under 256 residues at one tempera-
286   ture, so the clash statistic and seven observables do not validate all-atom stereochemistry, packing,
287   or energetics. Small panels. The sampling analysis rests on eight proteins and one released seed; an
288   eight-unit bootstrap is coarse, and the retention observables carry no multiplicity correction. Lim-
289   ited baselines. Only the pinned source and coordinate persistence are matched, and we name what
290   we did not run. Conditional ﬁxed-lag transition models [Klein et al., 2023; Schreiner et al., 2023;
291   Hsu et al., 2024] are the correct comparator class but are released for peptides and small molecules
292   rather than 256-residue domains, so matching requires retraining, not inference. Recent mdCATH-
293   trained trajectory models [Shen et al., 2025] report ensemble-level rather than exact-lag metrics, so
294   matching them requires re-implementing their evaluation under our start, sample-count, alignment
295   and split contract. Equilibrium emulators [Lewis et al., 2025; Jing et al., 2024] sample without a con-
296   ditioning start and are not applicable. Without matched runs, no ﬁeld-competitiveness conclusion is
297   available.

313   9 Conclusion

314   Keeping 7.35% of an exact-lag parent’s weight displacement improves endpoint prediction over
315   source M AR S-FM while passing a retention screen that every more aggressive adaptation failed.
316   Measured along ensemble size, 42% of the K = 4 gain turns out to be a 1/K dispersion term, while
317   the location component underneath strengthens and a strictly proper score favors the candidate at
318   every K. The gain is real, and it is between 0.014 and 0.033 Å depending on how it is measured—
319   which is why the next adaptation study of a molecular simulation foundation model should state its
320   anchoring strength and ensemble size before it states an improvement.

408   D Additional interpretation of retention metrics
409   The retention suite spans correlated motion (DCCM), ﬂuctuation magnitude (RMSF), global size
410   (radius of gyration), distributional spread (pairwise RMSD), low-dimensional covariance geometry
411   (RMSIP), contact preservation, and a coarse Cα clash statistic. These are complementary but incom-
412   plete. Similar RMSF can coexist with different cross-correlations, and similar radius-of-gyration
413   distributions can coexist with different local contacts. Requiring every observable to pass avoids
414   averaging a large regression in one property against an improvement in another. The gaps listed in
415   Section 7 are not ﬁlled by adding these observables together.
